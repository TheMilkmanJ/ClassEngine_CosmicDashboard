\title{If the lightest neutrino mass is the dark-energy scale:\\
a narrow window for neutrinoless double-beta decay}

\author{Justin Pulford}
\email{pulfordj420@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Unaffiliated}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The observed dark-energy density corresponds to an energy scale
$\rho_\Lambda^{1/4} \simeq 2.25\,$meV, close to the neutrino mass splittings. This numerical
proximity is long noted and has motivated a substantial model-building literature. We do not
propose a mechanism. Instead we take the sharpest arithmetic version of the coincidence as a
hypothesis --- that the lightest neutrino mass equals that scale, $m_1 = 2.25\,$meV, with normal
ordering --- and ask what it implies for neutrinoless double-beta decay. The mapping from
$m_{\rm lightest}$ to the effective Majorana mass is standard and the resulting band appears in
every review of the subject; what is specific here is the consequence of evaluating it at this one
value. With current oscillation parameters the three contributions are
$(1.52, 2.67, 1.10)\,$meV, giving $m_{\beta\beta} \in [0.04, 5.30]\,$meV and
$\Sigma m_\nu = 61.3\,$meV. The lower edge is not protected by any symmetry: it is nonzero only
because the middle term exceeds the other two combined by $0.045\,$meV, so the three phasors cannot
close. That same near-cancellation makes $m_{\beta\beta}$ an unusually steep function of $m_1$. The
observationally useful statement is the upper edge. Minimal normal ordering ($m_1 = 0$) cannot
exceed $3.69\,$meV at any phases, while this hypothesis reaches $5.30\,$meV and occupies the
interval between them $32\%$ of the time. The projected reach of a single planned experiment falls
inside that interval, so a detection there would be difficult to attribute to minimal ordering,
while a detection above $5.30\,$meV excludes the hypothesis outright. A null result constrains
nothing, at any sensitivity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The energy density of dark energy, expressed as a mass scale, is
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\Lambda^{1/4} \simeq 2.25\,\mathrm{meV},
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
%
which lies within an order of magnitude of $\sqrt{\Delta m^2_{21}} \simeq 8.6\,$meV and two orders
of $\sqrt{\Delta m^2_{31}} \simeq 50\,$meV. That the cosmological constant and the neutrino masses
should sit at comparable scales, when they are separated by every other consideration in the
Standard Model, has been remarked on for decades and has motivated a large body of model building
--- most directly the mass-varying-neutrino scenarios in which the dark-energy density is tied
dynamically to the neutrino sector~\cite{FardonNelsonWeiner2004,PecceiMaVaN2005}.

We take no position on any of those mechanisms, and we propose no new one. Our purpose is narrower
and, we think, more useful: the coincidence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho} admits one arithmetically sharp
reading, and that reading has consequences which are close to being tested. The reading is
%
\begin{equation}
  m_1 = \rho_\Lambda^{1/4} = 2.25\,\mathrm{meV}, \qquad \text{normal ordering},
  \label{eq:hyp}
\end{equation}
%
with $m_1$ the lightest neutrino mass. Everything below follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} together
with measured oscillation parameters. No cosmological model enters, and nothing we compute depends
on why Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} might hold.

\emph{What is and is not new here.} The map from the lightest mass to the effective Majorana mass
$m_{\beta\beta}$ is textbook, and the familiar lobster-shaped allowed region in the
$(m_{\rm lightest}, m_{\beta\beta})$ plane appears in every review of neutrinoless double-beta
decay~\cite{DellOroReview2016,AgostiniReview2023}. Evaluating it at one particular value of
$m_{\rm lightest}$ is not a new calculation. What we draw out is that this particular value lands
in an unusually informative place: it sits just above the reach of minimal normal ordering, and the
gap between the two is where one planned experiment is projected to look. That placement, and the
detection probabilities attached to it, is the content of this paper.

Section~\ref{sec:calc} fixes the inputs and computes the window. Section~\ref{sec:floor} explains
why the lower edge is nonzero and why it is fragile. Section~\ref{sec:test} sets out what would
distinguish the hypothesis from minimal ordering and what would refute it. Section~\ref{sec:assume}
states the assumptions.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The effective mass}\label{sec:calc}

For three Majorana neutrinos the amplitude for neutrinoless double-beta decay is controlled by
%
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    m_{\beta\beta} &= \Big| \sum_{i=1}^{3} U_{ei}^2\, m_i \Big| \\[2pt]
    &= \Big| c_{12}^2 c_{13}^2 m_1 + s_{12}^2 c_{13}^2 m_2 e^{i\alpha_2}
       + s_{13}^2 m_3 e^{i\alpha_3} \Big|,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:mbb}
\end{equation}
%
with $\alpha_2, \alpha_3$ the Majorana phases, which oscillation experiments do not constrain. We
use $\sin^2\theta_{12} = 0.307$, $\sin^2\theta_{13} = 0.022$,
$\Delta m^2_{21} = 7.42 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV}^2$ and
$\Delta m^2_{31} = 2.51 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{eV}^2$, consistent with current global
fits~\cite{NuFIT2020,PDG2024}. With $m_2 = (m_1^2 + \Delta m^2_{21})^{1/2}$ and
$m_3 = (m_1^2 + \Delta m^2_{31})^{1/2}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} gives
$m_2 = 8.90\,$meV, $m_3 = 50.15\,$meV, and
%
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma m_\nu = 61.3\,\mathrm{meV}.
  \label{eq:sum}
\end{equation}
%
The three contributions to Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbb} are
%
\begin{equation}
  \big( |U_{e1}^2 m_1|,\ |U_{e2}^2 m_2|,\ |U_{e3}^2 m_3| \big)
  = (1.52,\ 2.67,\ 1.10)\ \mathrm{meV}.
  \label{eq:terms}
\end{equation}
%
Varying the phases over their full range gives
%
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\ 0.04\,\mathrm{meV} \;\le\; m_{\beta\beta} \;\le\; 5.30\,\mathrm{meV}\ }
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
%
with $\simeq 3.3\,$meV typical. The hypothesis fixes $m_1$ but says nothing about the Majorana
phases, so position within Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} is unconstrained; that is a genuine limitation and
not a marginal one, as Sec.~\ref{sec:test} makes quantitative.

The mass sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sum} is the more immediately testable number. Current cosmological
bounds from the microwave background combined with baryon acoustic oscillations have reached
$\Sigma m_\nu \lesssim 72\,$meV, with some combinations pressing lower~\cite{DESI2024}. The value
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sum} sits just inside, so this hypothesis may well be graded by cosmology before
any laboratory experiment reaches it.

\section{Why the lower edge is nonzero, and why it is fragile}\label{sec:floor}

Complete cancellation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbb} requires the three complex terms to close a triangle,
which is possible only if no one of them exceeds the sum of the other two. From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:terms},
%
\begin{equation}
  2.67 \;>\; 1.52 + 1.10 = 2.62 \ \mathrm{meV},
  \label{eq:triangle}
\end{equation}
%
by $0.045\,$meV. The middle term wins, the triangle cannot close, and the lower edge of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} follows as the residue $2.67 - 2.62$.

Two things should be said about this immediately. First, the margin is $0.045\,$meV on terms of
order $2.6\,$meV --- a $1.7\%$ effect. Nothing protects it. It is not the consequence of a symmetry
and it would not survive a modest shift in $\theta_{12}$, in $\Delta m^2_{21}$, or in $m_1$ itself.
At $m_1 \gtrsim 2.32\,$meV the inequality reverses and the floor vanishes entirely.

Second, and more usefully, the same near-degeneracy makes $m_{\beta\beta}$ a steep function of
$m_1$ near this point. A quantity sitting close to a cancellation responds sharply to its inputs.
This is the one respect in which evaluating the standard band at this particular value is more than
a lookup: the hypothesis places $m_1$ where the effective mass is unusually sensitive to it.

We stress that no observable consequence rides on the lower edge. At $0.04\,$meV it is two orders
of magnitude below any experimental reach on any timeline under discussion. Every statement in
Sec.~\ref{sec:test} concerns the upper edge, which the near-cancellation barely moves.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{What would test it}\label{sec:test}

\subsection{The interval that separates hypotheses}

The comparison that matters is not against zero but against the minimal alternative. Setting
$m_1 = 0$ with the same ordering and parameters gives
%
\begin{equation}
  m_{\beta\beta}^{\rm min} \in [1.48,\ 3.69]\ \mathrm{meV},
  \label{eq:minimal}
\end{equation}
%
so minimal normal ordering has a hard ceiling at $3.69\,$meV that no choice of phases can exceed.
The hypothesis of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} reaches $5.30\,$meV. The interval
%
\begin{equation}
  3.69\,\mathrm{meV} \;<\; m_{\beta\beta} \;<\; 5.30\,\mathrm{meV}
  \label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
%
is therefore inaccessible to minimal ordering and occupied by this hypothesis. Integrating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbb} over flat Majorana phases, the hypothesis lands inside Eq.~\eqref{eq:band}
$31.8\%$ of the time.

\subsection{Where the experiments fall}

Projected ten-year reaches for the ton-scale programme, quoted as ranges because the nuclear matrix
element is the dominant uncertainty, are approximately $4.7$--$20.3\,$meV for
nEXO~\cite{nEXO2021}, $9$--$21\,$meV for LEGEND-1000~\cite{LEGEND1000}, and $12$--$34\,$meV for
CUPID~\cite{CUPID2019}.

Against the ceiling of Eq.~\eqref{eq:window}, LEGEND-1000 and CUPID lie entirely above the
hypothesis and cannot reach it for any matrix element. Only nEXO overlaps, and only at the
favourable end of its matrix-element range, $4.7$--$5.3\,$meV. Over flat phases the hypothesis
exceeds $4.7\,$meV $10.8\%$ of the time.

Two features of this placement are worth separating. The probability is low --- roughly one in nine
--- so the baseline experiment is unlikely to see a signal even if the hypothesis is correct. But
the entire $4.7$--$5.3\,$meV overlap lies inside the interval of Eq.~\eqref{eq:band}, which minimal
ordering cannot reach. A baseline nEXO detection would therefore be hard to accommodate within
minimal normal ordering, which is a stronger statement than the detection probability alone
suggests.

\subsection{Why a sensitivity upgrade does not help as much as expected}

A factor-of-four improvement in half-life sensitivity, of the kind a barium-tagging upgrade is
projected to deliver, corresponds to a factor $\sqrt{4} = 2$ in $m_{\beta\beta}$, taking the reach
to $\simeq 2.35\,$meV. The detection probability rises from $10.8\%$ to $69.1\%$.

This buys likelihood, not discrimination. Minimal normal ordering also exceeds $2.35\,$meV, and
does so $63.7\%$ of the time. A tagged detection near that threshold would be consistent with both
hypotheses and would separate almost nothing. The unimproved machine is the discriminating one,
precisely because its reach sits in the interval where the two hypotheses do not overlap.

\subsection{Falsification}

A measured $m_{\beta\beta} > 5.30\,$meV refutes Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} outright, since no choice of
phases can produce it. Evidence for inverted ordering does the same: at $m_3 = 2.25\,$meV inverted
ordering gives $m_{\beta\beta} \in [18.6, 49.1]\,$meV, which does not overlap
Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} at any phase. Evidence that neutrinos are Dirac particles removes the
observable entirely.

A null result refutes nothing. Because the phases are free, $m_{\beta\beta}$ can be arbitrarily
small, and no non-observation at any sensitivity constrains Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp}. This asymmetry is
intrinsic to the hypothesis and we make no attempt to dress it up.

For orientation, the strongest current limit comes from $^{136}$Xe: KamLAND-Zen reports
$T_{1/2} > 4.3 \times 10^{26}\,$yr at $90\%$ confidence over a $2097\,$kg\,yr exposure,
corresponding to $m_{\beta\beta} < 28$--$122\,$meV across the matrix-element
range~\cite{KamLANDZen2023}. That is a factor of five or more above the ceiling of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:window}, so no existing measurement bears on this hypothesis in either direction.

\section{What is assumed}\label{sec:assume}

\emph{Majorana neutrinos.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbb} presumes lepton-number violation. If neutrinos are
Dirac there is no $m_{\beta\beta}$ and the question does not arise.

\emph{Normal ordering.} Assumed throughout, and stated in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} rather than derived.
Global fits currently prefer it at modest significance; the preference is not settled.

\emph{Three light species.} A light sterile state would add a term to Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbb} and widen
the window.

\emph{Standard mechanism.} We assume light-neutrino exchange dominates the amplitude. If other
lepton-number-violating physics contributes, $m_{\beta\beta}$ as extracted from a half-life is no
longer the quantity computed here.

\emph{The numerical input.} We use the observed $\rho_\Lambda^{1/4} = 2.25\,$meV. Nothing depends
on this at the quoted precision: shifting it by $0.5\%$ moves the lower edge of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} from $0.045$ to $0.039\,$meV and leaves the upper edge at $5.30\,$meV, so
every conclusion in Sec.~\ref{sec:test} is unchanged.

\emph{And the coincidence itself.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:hyp} is a hypothesis, not a result. We are aware
that equating two scales because they are numerically close is weak grounds, and we do not argue
otherwise. The case for writing it down is that it is sharp, that it costs nothing to state, and
that it happens to be nearly testable --- not that the coincidence is evidence for itself.

\section{Conclusion}

Taking the lightest neutrino mass equal to the dark-energy scale, with normal ordering, gives
$m_{\beta\beta} \in [0.04, 5.30]\,$meV and $\Sigma m_\nu = 61.3\,$meV. The lower edge exists only
through a $0.045\,$meV margin in the triangle inequality and carries no observational weight. The
upper edge does the work.

The useful comparison is against minimal normal ordering, whose hard ceiling is $3.69\,$meV. The
interval between the two ceilings is closed to minimal ordering, is occupied by this hypothesis
about a third of the time, and contains the entire projected overlap of one planned experiment. A
detection there would be awkward to explain with minimal ordering; a detection above $5.30\,$meV
would refute the hypothesis; a null result would say nothing at all.

Cosmology will likely speak first. A mass sum of $61.3\,$meV sits just inside current combined
limits, and the bound is tightening.
